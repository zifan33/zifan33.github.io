Based on David Romer, “Advanced Macroeconomics,” 3rd edition, Chapter 1.

1 Overview

The Solow growth model is the workhorse of modern growth theory. It asks: what determines the long-run standard of living of a nation? The answer turns on capital accumulation, population growth, and technological progress — three forces that interact to pin down a unique, stable long-run equilibrium.

2 Setup and Assumptions

2.1 The Three Inputs

The economy produces a single good \(Y\) using three inputs:

  • Capital \(K(t)\): the stock of physical capital (machines, structures, equipment).
  • Labor \(L(t)\): the number of workers.
  • Technology \(A(t)\): the level of productive knowledge.

All variables are continuous functions of time \(t\).

2.2 Exogenous Growth of Labor and Technology

Both \(L\) and \(A\) grow at constant, exogenous rates:

\[ \dot{L}(t) = n \, L(t) \tag{1}\]

\[ \dot{A}(t) = g \, A(t) \tag{2}\]

Notation

\(\dot{x} \equiv dx/dt\) is the time derivative of \(x\).

\(n\) = population (labor) growth rate. Typical value: 1–2% per year.

\(g\) = rate of labor-augmenting technological progress. Typical value: 1–2% per year.

Integrating Equation 1 and Equation 2:

\[ L(t) = L(0)\,e^{nt}, \qquad A(t) = A(0)\,e^{gt} \]

Economic intuition: Population growth is treated as a pure demographic fact — exogenous to the model. Technological progress reflects cumulative human knowledge, also treated as exogenous here. This exogeneity is the model’s key limitation and the starting point for endogenous growth theory.

2.3 The Production Function

Output is:

\[ Y(t) = F\!\bigl(K(t),\; A(t)\,L(t)\bigr) \tag{3}\]

Notation

\(Y\) = aggregate output (GDP).

\(K\) = aggregate capital stock.

\(A(t)L(t)\) = “effective labor.” One worker with technology \(A\) is as productive as \(A\) workers at baseline technology.

This specification of technology as labor-augmenting (also called Harrod-neutral) is necessary for a balanced growth path to exist.

2.3.1 Assumption 1 — Constant Returns to Scale

For any scalar \(c > 0\):

\[ F(cK,\; cAL) = c\,F(K,\;AL) \tag{4}\]

Doubling all inputs exactly doubles output. The standard justification is replication: clone a factory together with all its workers, and output doubles.

2.3.2 Assumption 2 — Positive, Diminishing Marginal Products

\[ F_K > 0,\quad F_{AL} > 0; \qquad F_{KK} < 0,\quad F_{AL,AL} < 0 \tag{5}\]

More capital or more effective labor raises output, but at a declining rate. The first machine is very productive; the hundredth less so because it competes for the same workers.

2.3.3 Assumption 3 — Inada Conditions

\[ \lim_{K \to 0} F_K = \infty,\quad \lim_{K \to \infty} F_K = 0 \tag{6}\]

\[ \lim_{AL \to 0} F_{AL} = \infty,\quad \lim_{AL \to \infty} F_{AL} = 0 \tag{7}\]

Inada conditions guarantee that the \(sf(k)\) curve starts above the break-even line near \(k=0\) and falls below it for large \(k\), ensuring a unique interior steady state. Named after Ken-Ichi Inada (1963).

When capital is scarce, an additional unit is enormously valuable (infinite marginal product). When capital is abundant, extra units add almost nothing. These two boundary conditions, combined with continuity, pin down a unique positive steady state.

2.4 Capital Accumulation

Notation

\(s\) = saving rate, \(s \in (0,1)\). The fraction of output households save. Treated as exogenous and constant in the Solow model.

\(\delta\) = depreciation rate of capital. Typical value: 5–10% per year.

A fixed fraction \(s\) of output is saved and invested:

\[ \dot{K}(t) = s\,Y(t) - \delta\,K(t) \tag{8}\]

The capital stock rises by gross investment \(sY\) and falls by depreciation \(\delta K\).

3 The Intensive Form

3.1 Transforming Variables

Working in levels is cumbersome because \(K\), \(L\), and \(A\) all trend upward. Instead, divide by \(A(t)L(t)\) — the total number of effective workers — to obtain stationary variables:

\[ k(t) \;\equiv\; \frac{K(t)}{A(t)\,L(t)}, \qquad y(t) \;\equiv\; \frac{Y(t)}{A(t)\,L(t)} \tag{9}\]

Notation

\(k\) = capital per effective worker.

\(y\) = output per effective worker.

Dividing by \(AL\) (not just \(L\)) removes both population growth and technological growth, leaving a variable that is constant in the long run.

3.2 Intensive-Form Production Function

Apply the CRS property (Equation 4) with \(c = 1/(AL)\):

\[ F\!\left(\frac{K}{AL},\;1\right) = \frac{1}{AL}\,F(K,\,AL) = \frac{Y}{AL} = y \]

So we can write:

\[ y = f(k), \qquad \text{where } f(k) \equiv F(k,\,1) \tag{10}\]

The intensive-form function \(f\) inherits the properties of \(F\): \(f'>0\), \(f''<0\), \(f(0)=0\), with Inada conditions \(f'(0)=\infty\) and \(f'(\infty)=0\).

Cobb-Douglas example. If \(F(K,AL)=K^\alpha(AL)^{1-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), then \[f(k)=k^\alpha \tag{11}\] This is the most common functional form in applications.

3.3 Deriving the Equation of Motion for \(k\)

Differentiate the definition \(k = K/(AL)\) with respect to time (quotient rule):

\[ \dot{k} = \frac{\dot{K}}{AL} - \frac{K}{AL}\cdot\frac{d(AL)/dt}{AL} \]

The growth rate of \(AL\) is:

\[ \frac{d(AL)/dt}{AL} = \frac{\dot{A}L + A\dot{L}}{AL} = \frac{\dot{A}}{A} + \frac{\dot{L}}{L} = g + n \]

So:

\[ \dot{k} = \frac{\dot{K}}{AL} - (n+g)\,k \tag{12}\]

Substitute \(\dot{K} = sY - \delta K\) from Equation 8:

\[ \dot{k} = \frac{sY - \delta K}{AL} - (n+g)\,k = s\cdot\frac{Y}{AL} - \delta\cdot\frac{K}{AL} - (n+g)\,k \]

Using Equation 9 and Equation 10:

\[ \boxed{\dot{k} = s\,f(k) - (n + g + \delta)\,k} \tag{13}\]

Equation 13 is the engine of the Solow model. It fully describes how the economy evolves over time. All the model’s predictions — the steady state, convergence, comparative statics — follow directly from this single equation.

Term-by-term intuition for Equation 13:

Term Meaning
\(s\,f(k)\) Actual investment per effective worker: the fraction \(s\) of output that gets saved and added to capital
\(\delta\, k\) Capital lost to depreciation
\(n\,k\) Capital diluted by new workers joining the labor force
\(g\,k\) Capital diluted because each worker becomes more productive (the same \(K\) now serves a larger effective workforce)
\((n+g+\delta)k\) Total break-even investment: what must be invested just to keep \(k\) constant

\(k\) rises when actual investment exceeds break-even, and falls otherwise.

4 The Steady State

4.1 Existence and Uniqueness

A steady state is a value \(k^*>0\) at which \(\dot{k}=0\). Setting Equation 13 to zero:

\[ s\,f(k^*) = (n+g+\delta)\,k^* \tag{14}\]

The steady state \(k^*\) depends on \(s\), \(n\), \(g\), and \(\delta\). A country with a higher saving rate or lower population growth has a higher \(k^*\) and therefore a higher long-run standard of living.

Existence and uniqueness via Inada conditions. Define \(h(k) \equiv sf(k)/[(n+g+\delta)k] = sf(k)/k \cdot 1/(n+g+\delta)\). By the Inada conditions:

\[ \lim_{k\to 0} h(k) = \frac{s \cdot f'(0)}{n+g+\delta} = \infty, \qquad \lim_{k\to\infty} h(k) = \frac{s\cdot 0}{n+g+\delta} = 0 \]

Because \(h\) is continuous and strictly decreasing (since \(f''<0\)), there is exactly one \(k^*>0\) where \(h(k^*)=1\), i.e., where the two curves cross.

4.2 Stability

For \(k < k^*\): \(sf(k) > (n+g+\delta)k\) so \(\dot{k}>0\) (capital accumulates). For \(k > k^*\): \(sf(k) < (n+g+\delta)k\) so \(\dot{k}<0\) (capital decumulates).

Therefore \(k^*\) is globally stable: any economy starting at \(k(0)>0\) converges to \(k^*\).

4.3 Long-Run Growth Rates

Once the economy reaches \(k^*\), what grows and at what rate?

Variable Definition Growth rate at \(k^*\)
\(k = K/(AL)\) Capital per effective worker 0
\(y = Y/(AL)\) Output per effective worker 0
\(K/L\) Capital per worker \(g\)
\(Y/L\) Output per worker (living standard) \(g\)
\(Y\) Total output \(n+g\)

The central prediction: In the long run, output per worker grows at rate \(g\) — the rate of technology — regardless of the saving rate \(s\). Saving affects the level of output per worker, not its long-run growth rate.

Key insight. The saving rate \(s\) has no effect on the long-run growth rate of per-capita income. It only affects the level. This sharp prediction distinguishes the Solow model from models with linear production functions (AK models), where \(s\) permanently raises the growth rate.

5 Transition Dynamics and Convergence

5.1 Speed of Convergence

How fast does the economy approach \(k^*\)? Define the deviation \(\tilde{k}(t) \equiv k(t) - k^*\). Linearizing \(\dot{k} = \phi(k) \equiv sf(k)-(n+g+\delta)k\) around \(k^*\):

\[ \dot{\tilde{k}} \approx \phi'(k^*)\,\tilde{k} \tag{15}\]

where

\[ \phi'(k^*) = sf'(k^*) - (n+g+\delta) \tag{16}\]

Equation 15 is just a first-order linear ODE with constant coefficient \(\phi'(k^*)\). Separating variables and integrating:

\[ \frac{d\tilde{k}}{\tilde{k}} = \phi'(k^*)\,dt \;\implies\; \ln \tilde{k}(t) = \phi'(k^*)\,t + C \;\implies\; \tilde{k}(t) = \tilde{k}(0)\,e^{\phi'(k^*)\,t} \]

At a stable steady state, \(\phi'(k^*) < 0\) (the curve \(\dot{k}\) slopes downward at \(k^*\)), so this solution decays to zero — the economy returns to \(k^*\). We define \(\lambda\) simply to replace the negative coefficient with a positive one:

\[ \lambda \;\equiv\; -\phi'(k^*) > 0 \]

so the solution reads more naturally as exponential decay:

\[ \tilde{k}(t) \approx \tilde{k}(0)\,e^{-\lambda t} \tag{17}\]

\(\lambda > 0\) is the convergence rate: the fraction of the remaining gap closed per unit time. A larger \(\lambda\) means faster convergence.

It remains to compute \(\lambda\) explicitly. Define \(\alpha_K^* \equiv k^*f'(k^*)/f(k^*)\) as the capital share at the steady state. The steady-state condition \(sf(k^*) = (n+g+\delta)k^*\) implies:

\[ s = \frac{(n+g+\delta)\,k^*}{f(k^*)} \tag{18}\]

Substituting into \(\lambda = (n+g+\delta) - sf'(k^*)\):

\[ \lambda = (n+g+\delta) - \frac{(n+g+\delta)\,k^*}{f(k^*)}\cdot f'(k^*) = (n+g+\delta)\!\left(1 - \frac{k^*f'(k^*)}{f(k^*)}\right) = (n+g+\delta)\bigl(1 - \alpha_K^*\bigr) \tag{19}\]

The economy closes a fraction \(1-e^{-\lambda t}\) of the gap each period.

Numerical example. With \(\alpha_K^* = 1/3\), \(n=0.01\), \(g=0.02\), \(\delta=0.05\):

\[ \lambda = (0.01+0.02+0.05)\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{3}\bigr) = 0.08 \times \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 5.3\% \]

Half-life \(= \ln 2 / \lambda \approx 13\) years. If human capital is included and \(\alpha_K^* \approx 2/3\), then \(\lambda \approx 2.7\%\) and the half-life is about 26 years, which matches cross-country empirical estimates better.

5.2 Absolute vs. Conditional Convergence

  • Absolute convergence: all countries converge to the same \(k^*\). Requires identical \(s\), \(n\), \(g\), \(\delta\), and technology — unrealistic.
  • Conditional convergence: each country converges to its own \(k^*\) determined by its parameters. Countries below their own steady state grow faster. This is what cross-country data support: controlling for steady-state determinants, poorer countries grow faster.

6 Comparative Statics

6.1 A Rise in the Saving Rate \(s\)

Suppose \(s\) rises permanently from \(s_1\) to \(s_2 > s_1\).

Step 1. The break-even line \((n+g+\delta)k\) is unchanged. The actual investment curve shifts up. A new (higher) steady state \(k_2^* > k_1^*\) emerges from Equation 14.

Step 2. At the moment of the shock, \(k = k_1^*\) but \(s_2 f(k_1^*) > (n+g+\delta)k_1^*\), so \(\dot{k} > 0\) and capital accumulation resumes.

Step 3. Output per effective worker rises from \(y_1^* = f(k_1^*)\) to \(y_2^* = f(k_2^*) > y_1^*\).

Step 4. The long-run growth rate of \(Y/L\) remains \(g\) — higher \(s\) only raises the level.

6.2 A Rise in Population Growth \(n\)

A higher \(n\) steepens the break-even line. The actual investment curve \(sf(k)\) is unchanged, so the intersection moves to the left: \(k^{**} < k^*\).

\[ (n_2 + g + \delta)k^{**} = sf(k^{**}), \quad n_2 > n_1 \implies k^{**} < k^* \tag{20}\]

Higher population growth lowers steady-state capital and output per effective worker. The model predicts a negative cross-country correlation between \(n\) and per-capita income, consistent with the data.

7 The Golden Rule

7.1 Maximizing Steady-State Consumption

The steady state \(k^*\) is pinned down by \(s\), but which steady state maximizes consumption per effective worker? Steady-state consumption is:

\[ c^* = y^* - i^* = f(k^*) - sf(k^*) \tag{21}\]

Using Equation 14, \(sf(k^*) = (n+g+\delta)k^*\), so:

\[ c^* = f(k^*) - (n+g+\delta)\,k^* \tag{22}\]

Equation 22 has a clean geometric interpretation: \(c^*\) is the vertical gap between the production function \(f(k^*)\) and the break-even line \((n+g+\delta)k^*\). Maximizing \(c^*\) is equivalent to maximizing this gap.

To maximize \(c^*\) over \(k^*\), differentiate with respect to \(k^*\) and set to zero:

\[ \frac{dc^*}{dk^*} = f'(k^*) - (n+g+\delta) = 0 \]

\[ \boxed{f'(k_{\text{gold}}) = n + g + \delta} \tag{23}\]

This is the Golden Rule of capital accumulation. The optimal long-run capital stock equates the marginal product of capital to the effective depreciation rate.

Economic intuition. Investing one more unit of capital raises output by \(f'(k)\) but costs \((n+g+\delta)\) per period to maintain (depreciation plus dilution from growth). At the Golden Rule, these exactly balance. Below \(k_\text{gold}\), the return exceeds the cost — invest more. Above \(k_\text{gold}\), the cost exceeds the return — invest less.

7.2 The Golden Rule Saving Rate

The Golden Rule saving rate \(s_\text{gold}\) is the value of \(s\) that makes the model’s steady state equal to \(k_\text{gold}\). Using Equation 14 at \(k_\text{gold}\):

\[ s_\text{gold} = \frac{(n+g+\delta)\,k_\text{gold}}{f(k_\text{gold})} \tag{24}\]

For Cobb-Douglas (Equation 11), \(f'(k)=\alpha k^{\alpha-1}\), so Equation 23 gives \(\alpha k_\text{gold}^{\alpha-1} = n+g+\delta\). Using this in Equation 24:

\[ s_\text{gold} = \frac{f'(k_\text{gold})\,k_\text{gold}}{f(k_\text{gold})} = \frac{\alpha\,k_\text{gold}^\alpha}{k_\text{gold}^\alpha} = \alpha \]

A remarkable result: for Cobb-Douglas, the Golden Rule saving rate equals the capital share \(\alpha\). If \(\alpha \approx 1/3\), then \(s_\text{gold} \approx 33\%\).

7.3 Dynamic Inefficiency

Compare the economy’s actual steady state \(k^*\) to the Golden Rule \(k_\text{gold}\):

  • \(k^* < k_\text{gold}\) (under-accumulation): Raising \(s\) increases \(k^*\) toward \(k_\text{gold}\) and eventually raises consumption. But there is a short-run cost — higher saving means less consumption today. This is a genuine intertemporal trade-off.
  • \(k^* > k_\text{gold}\) (over-accumulation, dynamic inefficiency): Reducing \(s\) raises consumption immediately and permanently — a true free lunch. No one needs to sacrifice. Empirical evidence suggests most countries have \(k^* < k_\text{gold}\), so dynamic inefficiency is not a practical concern for the Solow model.

8 Summary of Key Results

Result Statement
Long-run growth \(Y/L\) grows at rate \(g\) (technology); \(s\) has no effect on growth
Effect of \(s\uparrow\) Raises level of \(k^*\) and \(y^*\); no change in long-run growth rate
Effect of \(n\uparrow\) Lowers \(k^*\) and \(y^*\)
Convergence Economy converges to \(k^*\) at rate \(\lambda = (n+g+\delta)(1-\alpha_K^*)\)
Golden Rule \(c^*\) maximized when \(f'(k^*) = n+g+\delta\)
Dynamic efficiency Overcapitalization (\(k^*>k_\text{gold}\)) is Pareto-dominated

The Solow model’s greatest achievement is showing that capital accumulation alone cannot sustain long-run growth — only technological progress can. Its greatest limitation is that the rate of technological progress \(g\) is left entirely unexplained. This motivates the Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans model (which endogenizes saving) and later endogenous growth models (which endogenize \(g\)).
