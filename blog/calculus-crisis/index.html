How did mathematics produce correct answers from logically incoherent foundations

1 Overview

In the late 17th century, Newton and Leibniz independently invented calculus and handed humanity one of the most powerful intellectual tools ever devised. Within decades, it was being applied to planetary orbits, fluid mechanics, and the shapes of hanging chains — all with stunning success.

There was, however, a problem. Nobody could say with any rigor what the fundamental objects of calculus were. The method worked, but the justification was, at its core, a sleight of hand: quantities were treated as nonzero when it was convenient, then discarded as zero when that became convenient. The contradiction was obvious and embarrassing. For roughly 150 years, calculus rested on foundations that its own practitioners could not defend.

This is the story of that crisis, the famous attack on it, and the hard-won resolution.

2 The Mechanism: What Calculus Actually Did

2.1 Newton’s Fluxions

Newton framed calculus in terms of motion. A variable quantity — a “fluent” — flows continuously through time. Its fluxion is its instantaneous rate of flow, the velocity of the quantity. To find the fluxion of \(x^2\), Newton would let \(x\) increase by a small amount \(o\) over a small interval of time, compute the change in \(x^2\), and then divide by \(o\):

\[ \frac{(x + o)^2 - x^2}{o} = \frac{2xo + o^2}{o} = 2x + o \]

Then Newton would set \(o = 0\) to obtain the fluxion \(2x\).

The result is correct. But the reasoning is not: \(o\) was assumed nonzero to justify the division, then assumed zero to discard the \(o^2/o = o\) term. Both assumptions cannot hold simultaneously.

2.2 Leibniz’s Differentials

Leibniz developed an equivalent but notationally different approach. He worked with differentials \(dx\) and \(dy\) — infinitely small increments in \(x\) and \(y\). The derivative is the ratio \(dy/dx\). For \(y = x^2\):

\[ dy = (x + dx)^2 - x^2 = 2x\,dx + (dx)^2 \]

Dividing by \(dx\):

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x + dx \]

Then \((dx)^2\) and the remaining \(dx\) were dropped as “infinitely small compared to the finite \(2x\),” leaving \(dy/dx = 2x\).

Again: correct answer, incoherent argument. If \(dx \neq 0\), the answer is \(2x + dx\), not \(2x\). If \(dx = 0\), the original division by \(dx\) is illegal.

2.3 The Pattern

Both methods share the same logical structure:

  1. Introduce a small quantity (\(o\) or \(dx\)) and assume it is nonzero — so division by it is valid.
  2. Expand and simplify.
  3. Discard all remaining powers of the small quantity as negligible.
  4. Obtain the correct answer.

Step 3 is the scandal. The practitioner must decide, by feel, which terms to keep and which to throw away. There is no principled rule — just the implicit understanding that “higher-order infinitesimals are smaller than first-order ones.” What does smaller mean for a quantity that is already infinitely small? Nobody said.

3 The Attack: Bishop Berkeley’s The Analyst

3.1 The Man and the Motive

George Berkeley (1685–1753), Bishop of Cloyne, was one of the great empiricist philosophers. He had a long-standing grievance with the scientific establishment: mathematicians and natural philosophers had been using their air of precision to attack religious faith, arguing that rigorous rational inquiry had no room for miracles or revelation. Berkeley decided to return the favor.

In 1734 he published The Analyst: A Discourse Addressed to an Infidel Mathematician. His target was almost certainly Edmond Halley (of Halley’s comet fame). His argument was devastating.

3.2 The Central Critique

Berkeley accepted that the results of calculus were often correct. His attack was on the reasoning. He argued that the logical status of fluxions and infinitesimals was no better — and arguably worse — than the “mysteries” of religion that mathematicians mocked.

His most famous line:

“And what are these Fluxions? The Velocities of evanescent Increments. And what are these same evanescent Increments? They are neither finite Quantities nor Quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the Ghosts of departed Quantities?”

The phrase ghosts of departed quantities is one of the most incisive epigrams in the history of science. It captures the problem exactly: the increments are invoked as real quantities when needed for the algebra, then made to vanish when they become inconvenient. They are neither alive (nonzero) nor dead (zero) — they haunt the calculation.

Berkeley further argued that the correct results were obtained not because of the reasoning, but despite it — specifically, because two errors happened to cancel each other out:

“By virtue of a twofold mistake you arrive, though not at science, yet at the truth.”

Berkeley’s deeper philosophical point was that mathematicians demanded clarity in others’ domains (theology) that they conspicuously lacked in their own. The rhetorical force of The Analyst came precisely from this hypocrisy.

3.3 A Concrete Example of the Double Error

Take the derivative of \(y = x^2\) using the increment method. Berkeley’s “twofold error” argument:

  • Error 1: Treat \(o\) as nonzero when dividing: \(\frac{(x+o)^2 - x^2}{o} = 2x + o\)
  • Error 2: Drop the \(o\) term at the end, as if \(o = 0\)

Each step is individually wrong, but the two errors cancel: the first “error” introduces a surplus \(o\), and the second “error” removes exactly that surplus, leaving the correct \(2x\). Berkeley’s point was that this looks like correct reasoning only because of a lucky coincidence in the algebra — and that no general principle justified why it always worked.

4 The Defense: Why Mathematicians Pressed On

4.1 The Pragmatic Argument

Faced with Berkeley’s critique, most mathematicians responded with something like: the method works; figure out the foundations later. This was not entirely unreasonable. Calculus was producing correct and verifiable predictions about the physical world at a rate that had never been seen before. Abandoning it because of a philosophical objection would have been scientifically catastrophic.

There is a recurring pattern in mathematics where powerful techniques are used for decades or centuries before they are properly understood. (Complex numbers followed a similar arc: routinely used by the 16th century, not rigorously understood until the 19th.)

4.2 Attempts at Rigorous Defense

Several mathematicians attempted to answer Berkeley on his own terms:

  • Colin Maclaurin (Treatise of Fluxions, 1742) tried to recast Newton’s calculus in the language of classical Greek geometry, avoiding infinitesimals entirely. The result was technically defensible but impossibly cumbersome and did not spread.

  • Jean le Rond d’Alembert argued that the derivative should be understood as a limit — the value approached by the difference quotient as the increment tends to zero. This was the germ of the right idea, but d’Alembert had no rigorous definition of “limit” and no way to make the argument precise.

  • Leonhard Euler simply treated infinitesimals as exactly zero when convenient. His results were almost always correct (Euler had preternatural instincts), but his justification satisfied no one who was paying attention.

None of these responses answered Berkeley rigorously. The foundations remained contested.

5 The Resolution: Limits and the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) Definition

5.1 Cauchy’s Reconstruction

The decisive step was taken by Augustin-Louis Cauchy in his Cours d’analyse (1821). Cauchy systematically rebuilt calculus on the concept of a limit, defined informally as follows:

A variable quantity \(u_n\) has limit \(\ell\) if, for any desired precision, the values of \(u_n\) eventually stay within that precision of \(\ell\).

He defined the derivative of \(f\) at \(x\) as:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]

The key move: \(h\) never equals zero in this definition. The limit describes the behavior of the difference quotient as \(h\) approaches zero, not at \(h = 0\). No quantity is ever simultaneously nonzero and zero. The ghost is exorcised.

Cauchy also gave the first rigorous definitions of continuity and of the definite integral (as the limit of Riemann sums), constructing the whole edifice from the limit concept upward.

5.2 Weierstrass’s Formalization

Cauchy’s limits still relied on intuitive language (“approaches,” “eventually”). Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897) replaced this with purely logical, quantifier-based definitions — eliminating all appeal to motion or intuition.

The \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) definition of a limit:

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \quad \iff \quad \forall\, \varepsilon > 0,\; \exists\, \delta > 0 \text{ such that } 0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon \]

The \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) definition replaced vague words like “approaches” with hard inequalities. It turned the limit from a dynamic process into a static logical statement — one that can be verified or refuted without any reference to time, motion, or “infinitely small” quantities.

This definition contains no infinitesimals, no motion, no intuition. Every term is a real number; every statement is a logical condition. With this, the derivative is:

\[ f'(a) = L \iff \forall\,\varepsilon > 0,\; \exists\,\delta > 0 \text{ such that } 0 < |h| < \delta \implies \left|\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} - L\right| < \varepsilon \]

For \(f(x) = x^2\), \(a = x\):

\[ \left|\frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} - 2x\right| = \left|2x + h - 2x\right| = |h| \]

So for any \(\varepsilon > 0\), choose \(\delta = \varepsilon\). Then \(0 < |h| < \delta\) implies \(|h| < \varepsilon\). The limit is \(2x\), rigorously. No ghosts anywhere.

5.3 What Changed

Era Foundation Status of \(dx\)
Newton / Leibniz Fluxions / differentials Nonzero then zero — inconsistent
Cauchy (1821) Limits (intuitive) Eliminated; limit is about approach, not arrival
Weierstrass (1860s) \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) Eliminated; purely static logical condition

The 150-year crisis was resolved by changing the question. Instead of asking “what is an infinitely small quantity?”, Weierstrass asked “when does a function’s value get arbitrarily close to a target?” The second question has a precise answer that requires no new ontology.

6 Epilogue: The Rehabilitation of Infinitesimals

6.1 Non-Standard Analysis

The story has a surprising coda. In 1960, logician Abraham Robinson proved that it is possible to extend the real numbers to a larger ordered field — the hyperreals — that genuinely contains infinitely small (and infinitely large) elements, and that this extension is logically consistent (assuming standard set theory is consistent).

In Robinson’s non-standard analysis, \(dx\) is a genuine nonzero infinitesimal, and \(dy/dx = 2x\) follows rigorously without epsilon-delta arguments. The intuitions of Newton and Leibniz were, in a deep sense, vindicated: infinitesimals can be made rigorous. Berkeley’s objection was not that infinitesimals were the wrong idea, but that they had not been defined properly.

Robinson’s hyperreals satisfy the transfer principle: any first-order statement true of the reals is also true of the hyperreals. This is what makes non-standard analysis consistent — it is not a different mathematics, but an extension of the same one.

The \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) approach and non-standard analysis give identical results. They are two different foundations for the same edifice. Today, \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) dominates in university courses, largely for historical and pedagogical reasons — not because infinitesimals are wrong, but because Weierstrass got there first.

7 The Broader Lesson

The calculus crisis illustrates a recurring feature of mathematical history: the engine of discovery and the engine of justification run at different speeds. Practitioners developed extraordinarily powerful methods — differentiation, integration, differential equations — decades or centuries before anyone could explain why those methods worked.

This is not a flaw unique to early modern mathematics. The same pattern appeared with:

  • Negative numbers (used routinely in accounting centuries before they were accepted as genuine numbers)
  • Complex numbers (used to solve cubic equations by Cardano in 1545; properly understood via the Argand plane only in the 1800s)
  • The Dirac delta “function” (used ubiquitously in physics from the 1920s; given rigorous foundations via distribution theory by Schwartz in 1950)

Berkeley’s critique was entirely correct on its own terms. The mathematicians who ignored it and kept computing were also correct to do so. The resolution — Cauchy and Weierstrass — showed that the right response to a foundational crisis is not to stop calculating, but to find the deeper concept that the calculation was already implicitly relying on.

In the case of calculus, that concept was the limit. It had been lurking in d’Alembert’s vague gestures and in Newton’s own phrase “ultimate ratio” — the ratio to which the difference quotient ultimately tends. It took another century to say what ultimately means.
